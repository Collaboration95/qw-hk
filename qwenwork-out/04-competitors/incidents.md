## 1. Singapore — the 2019 "technical recession" scare built on flash estimates later revised

**Dates:** Jul–Nov 2019.
**What happened:** MTI's July 2019 advance estimate showed Q2 GDP contracting 3.4% q/q annualised; the August final print (−3.3%) still triggered a slash of the 2019 growth forecast from 1.5–2.5% to 0–1% and widespread recession headlines. By October, Q2 had been revised again to −2.7%, and the Q3 flash (+0.6% q/q annualised, 0.1% y/y) produced "Singapore narrowly avoids technical recession" coverage. The November final estimate then revised Q3 up to 0.5% y/y (+2.1% q/q annualised) and Q2 y/y from 0.1% to 0.2% — the recession narrative had been based on superseded numbers.
**Revision magnitude:** Q2 q/q SAAR: −3.4% → −3.3% → −2.7%; Q3 y/y: 0.1% → 0.5%.
**Consequence:** forecast cuts, recession-fear coverage and market/policy expectations (MAS easing bets) driven by figures later revised away; the Straits Times explicitly noted the flash estimate had pointed to "narrowly avoiding a technical recession." Every newsletter and board paper written in Aug–Oct 2019 citing "Singapore is in/near technical recession" needed the exact still-true/now-wrong judgment Recall Press makes.

## 2. Singapore — Q3 2025: advance estimate badly understated growth; forecast upgraded (the headline case)

**Dates:** 14 Oct 2025 (advance) → 21 Nov 2025 (final).
**What happened:** MTI's advance estimate put Q3 2025 GDP at 2.9% y/y, with **manufacturing flat (0.0% y/y**, slowing from Q2's 5.0%). The final estimate revised Q3 growth to **4.2% y/y** (Q2 also revised 4.5% → 4.7%), beating the 4.0% Reuters poll, and MTI upgraded the full-year 2025 forecast from "1.5–2.5%" to "around 4.0%". **Manufacturing's final y/y growth was ~5.0%** ("5 per cent" per Business Times; +11.3% q/q SA) — a +5 pp sectoral swing from "flat" to "booming", driven by AI-related electronics (electronics +6.1%).
**Revision magnitude:** +1.3 pp on Q3 headline y/y (2.9% → 4.2%); +5 pp on manufacturing y/y (0.0% → ~5.0%); full-year forecast range roughly doubled.
**Consequence:** a materially wrong official first read of the economy; the October MAS policy statement and market commentary were made against the weaker advance picture. Any client document asserting "manufacturing stalled in Q3" between 14 Oct and 21 Nov was wrong by 21 Nov; "as first reported in October, manufacturing was flat" remained true.

## 3. Philippines — PSA revises Q4 2018 GDP after the figure was already public

**Date:** 4 Apr 2019.
**What happened:** PSA revised Q4 2018 GDP growth upward from 6.1% to 6.3%, citing trade, public administration and social security sectors. The 6.1% print had been published in January 2019 and used in public discourse on whether the economy was slowing. Full-year 2018 growth was kept at 6.2%.
**Revision magnitude:** +0.2 pp (6.1% → 6.3%).
**Consequence:** modest but documented correction of an already-cited official figure; PSA stressed revisions are routine practice. A larger related revision — Q1 2020 growth revised down from −0.2% to −0.7% — appears in PSA's own Facebook post *[search-only]* (https://www.facebook.com/PSAgovph/posts/3102916683131451/); no openable news article verified it.
**Source (opened):** https://www.gmanetwork.com/news/money/economy/690245/psa-revises-q4-gdp-higher-to-6-3-from-6-1/story/

## 4. Greece — deficit revisions 2009–2010 triggered the sovereign debt crisis

**Dates:** Oct 2009; 22 Apr 2010; 15 Nov 2010.
**What happened:** the incoming Papandreou government revealed in October 2009 that the deficit was ~12.5% of GDP, not the 3.7% projected by the previous government. Eurostat's April 2010 notification put the 2009 deficit at 13.6%; in November 2010 it was revised again to 15.4%, with debt revised from 115.1% to 126.8% of GDP. Each revision forced harder austerity terms on the €110bn bailout programme.
**Revision magnitude:** 3.7% → ~12.5% → 13.6% → 15.4% of GDP (deficit); debt 115.1% → 126.8%.
**Consequence:** Fitch downgraded Greece two days after the October 2009 revision (to A−, then BBB+ in December); S&P and Moody's followed; bond spreads blew out; the revisions repeatedly "threw out" the agreed fiscal programme and deepened austerity and social unrest. The archetypal case of unreliable official statistics causing a policy catastrophe — every analyst note citing the old deficit needed rewriting.
**Sources (opened):**
- https://www.esm.europa.eu/publications/safeguarding-euro/runaway-train-greece-sounds-alarm (ESM official history, with Eurostat/Fitch footnotes)
- https://www.theguardian.com/business/2010/nov/15/greek-deficit-bigger-than-thought

## 5. UK — ONS revisions erased the 2012 "double-dip recession"

**Date:** 27 Jun 2013.
**What happened:** ONS's Blue Book revisions changed Q1 2012 GDP from a 0.1% contraction to flat growth, meaning the widely reported 2012 double-dip recession never happened. The same revisions deepened the 2008–09 recession: peak-to-trough fall revised from 6.3% to 7.2%, leaving Q1 2013 output 3.9% below peak (previously 2.6%).
**Revision magnitude:** Q1 2012: −0.1% → 0.0%; 2008–09 peak-to-trough: −6.3% → −7.2%.
**Consequence:** a year of political and media narrative about a "double-dip" (and austerity debates framed by it) was invalidated; ONS's Joe Grice conceded small quarterly changes "don't affect the overall story," while analysts noted recovery was further away than thought.
**Source (opened):** https://www.bbc.com/news/business-23079082

## 6. US — the 2022 "technical recession" was revised out of existence

**Dates:** Apr–Jul 2022 (initial prints); 26 Sep 2024 (BEA annual update).
**What happened:** BEA initially reported negative annualised GDP in Q1 2022 (−1.6%) and Q2 2022 (advance −0.9%), prompting widespread "technical recession" claims and a White House pushback over the definition. In September 2024, BEA's annual revision flipped Q2 2022 growth from negative to positive. NYT's Ben Casselman: "Remember the 'technical recession'… Turns out it never happened."
**Revision magnitude:** Q2 2022: negative → positive (exact revised value not stated in the opened article; not quoted). Initial −1.6%/−0.9% prints per contemporaneous reporting *[search-only]*.
**Consequence:** two years of political argument about whether the US was in recession rested on data later shown to be wrong; a cautionary tale about treating advance GDP prints as definitive.

## 7. Japan — 2014 GDP revisions drove a tax-hike delay and a snap election

**Dates:** 8 Sep 2014; 17 Nov 2014; 8 Dec 2014.
**What happened:** Q2 2014 GDP was revised from −6.8% to −7.1% annualised (worse than the −7.0% poll), deepening the post-consumption-tax-hit contraction. Then Q3's surprise −1.6% print (vs +2.2% expected) confirmed recession; it was later revised further down to −1.9%. The November −1.6% reading directly led PM Abe to delay the second consumption-tax hike (to 10%) by 18 months and call a snap December election.
**Revision magnitude:** Q2: −6.8% → −7.1%; Q3: −1.6% → −1.9% (annualised).
**Consequence:** major fiscal-policy reversal and an election triggered by figures that were themselves subsequently revised; Brookings noted the dismal prints "made his calculation easier."

## 8. Argentina — IMF censure over manipulated inflation statistics

**Date:** 1 Feb 2013 (censure); reported 4 Feb 2013.
**What happened:** the IMF formally censured Argentina — the first such censure over economic data — for publishing inaccurate inflation and GDP statistics. The government claimed inflation below 11% while private economists estimated roughly double (some saw 30% ahead). Understated inflation had kept debt-service payments low and flattered the government. The IMF threatened exclusion from IMF decision-making if statistics weren't fixed by late September 2013; the censure was only lifted in November 2016 after a new CPI was introduced.
**Revision magnitude:** official <11% vs independent ~20–25% inflation (per BBC report).
**Consequence:** international institutional sanction, loss of statistical credibility, and a supermarket price freeze announced days after the censure. (This is manipulation rather than routine revision — included as the extreme end of the "official figures can't be trusted blindly" spectrum.)

## 9. India — 2026 GDP base revisions spark "fudging" accusations against the government

**Date:** Aug–Sep 2026 (reported 2 Sep 2026).
**What happened:** after official Q1 FY2026-27 growth of 7.8% was celebrated by PM Modi as a "herculean feat", former finance secretary Subhash Chandra Garg pointed out that the prior-year Q1 base had been revised down (real growth 7.8% → 6.9%; nominal GDP ₹86.05tn → ~₹80tn), mechanically boosting the current growth rate. Congress accused the government of "statistical gymnastics" and "fudging"; Garg's alternative nominal comparison gave 2.6%. Analysts noted his arithmetic on the revision was correct but mixed vintages and nominal vs real measures; MoSPI defended the revisions as standard methodology.
**Revision magnitude:** prior-year Q1 nominal GDP cut ~₹6tn (~7%); real growth 7.8% → 6.9%.
**Consequence:** a sitting government's headline achievement publicly undermined by its own former finance secretary; opposition parliamentary attack citing the revised base; statistics ministry forced to defend methodology publicly. A live demonstration that **base-year revisions silently change every growth rate computed against them** — exactly the dependency Recall Press would trace.

## What the incidents show

1. **Revisions are routine, not exotic** — Singapore alone produced two multi-point narrative-changing revisions in six years (2019, 2025), and the 2025 full-year figure was revised *again* in Feb 2026.
2. **The damage is narrative, not just numeric** — "technical recession" (Singapore 2019, US 2022), "double-dip" (UK 2012), "herculean feat" (India 2026): the sentences built on early prints outlive the prints.
3. **Corrections are mostly silent or absent** — no formal errata were found for statistic-revision cases; AP/Reuters policy exists but is human-triggered. The gap Recall Press fills is detecting the dependency *before* someone notices.
4. **Policy stakes are real** — Japan delayed a tax hike and called an election on a print later revised; Greece's revisions deepened a sovereign crisis; MAS policy commentary in Oct 2025 ran against the weaker advance picture.
